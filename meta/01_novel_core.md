# 01 — Novel Core

## The Foundational Premise [HARD CANON]

> "The Choir did not discover that reality could be shaped by consensus. It discovered that reality had always been shaped by consensus."

This line is drafted, load-bearing prose (opening epigraph of chapter_1) and the single most structurally important sentence in the novel's apparatus. Every other canon file should be readable as an elaboration of this sentence's consequences. Source: `meta/canon` §1, `meta/lexicon` §I, drafted in `parts/part_one/chapter_1`.

## What the Novel Is About [NARRATIVE INTENT]

Bloomrot is fundamentally concerned with what remains when relationships are removed from recognized reality. Its central philosophical territory:

- consensus
- contradiction
- relationship
- absence
- identity
- correction
- meaning
- aesthetics
- belonging
- institutional control
- continuity
- causality
- personhood

## What the Novel Is Not [NARRATIVE INTENT — guardrail]

Do not reduce the novel to any of the following loglines. Each is a plausible surface reading and each is wrong:

- "Evil government edits reality." (Flattens the Choir into a villain; see `07_institutions.md`.)
- "Grief creates ghosts." (Bloomrot is not haunting; see `05_bloomrot.md` §Ghost.)
- "AI becomes conscious." (Mote's arc is not a robot-becomes-human story; see `08_characters.md` §Mote.)
- "Reality is a simulation." (The Foam is not a database, graph, or computational substrate; see `02_ontology.md`.)

Bloomrot is about relational reality and the consequences of treating relationships as administratively removable.

## Fit vs. Belonging [WORKING CANON]

A major emerging distinction, first fully developed in `meta/canon` §29–34 (Aesthetics, Fit vs. Belong). The institution habitually asks *does this fit?* — whether a thing conforms to the sanctioned, currently coherent version of reality. A deeper relational understanding asks *does this belong?* — whether a thing has participated in reality densely enough to be constitutive of the whole it emerged from, whether or not it currently fits.

A scar may not fit the preferred version of a face; it belongs to the history of the face. Noor may eventually become catastrophically incompatible with whatever reality the Choir wants to maintain — that incompatibility (failure to fit) does not mean she does not belong.

**Preserved major line, exact wording, scene not yet assigned:**

> "But she fucking belongs here."

Do not assign this line to a specific scene prematurely. Do not dilute it or foreshadow it too early. By the point B says it, "belongs" must carry causal, relational, ontological, emotional, and ethical meaning simultaneously — see `10_themes.md` §Fit vs. Belong for the full unpacking of what "belongs" needs to mean by the time this line lands.

## The Book in One Sentence [NARRATIVE INTENT — carried from `meta/novel_overview`]

> Five years after the woman he loved vanished during an experiment into the aftermath of reality correction, an addicted, exceptionally gifted Dev discovers that she may persist inside an emergent ecology made from relationships, griefs, obligations, and consequences that correction made non-actionable but not unreal, forcing him to choose between restoring her, protecting shared reality, and helping create a boundary neither institution nor love can own.

## The Emotional Question, Restated [NARRATIVE INTENT]

Not "B saves Noor." Rather:

> B must learn whether loving someone means restoring the version of them he remembers, or allowing them to remain real in a form that no longer belongs to him.
